How the score is built.

Every number on the dashboard comes from a documented rule. This page describes the composite, the ten indicators, the point-in-time lags, the confidence gates, and the ways the result can be wrong.

The composite is a relative-attractiveness description, not a forecast. It does not tell you when to buy or sell, and it does not account for your time horizon, taxes, or risk capacity.

1. The composite

Four category scores are combined with fixed weights. Each category is itself a coverage-weighted average of its available indicators. Missing indicators are excluded and the remaining weights are renormalized, so a missing input lowers coverage rather than creating a fake neutral reading.

Category Overall weight What it measures
Fundamental valuation 0.50 Long-term valuation relative to earnings.
Relative attractiveness 0.20 Compensation offered by equities versus Treasuries.
Sentiment & momentum 0.20 Short-term fear, volatility, momentum, and trend.
Market breadth 0.10 Participation and concentration inside the index.

A composite score is published only when at least two categories are available and weighted coverage is at least 60%. Otherwise the dashboard shows “Insufficient data.”

2. Indicator weights and scoring direction

A percentile score answers: “Where does today’s reading sit within this indicator’s own rolling history?” A higher score means the current reading is more favorable for long-term buyers under the stated direction. Percentile windows default to 20 years, with shorter windows flagged when history is limited.

The 200-day deviation and equal-weight breadth are trend-oriented: higher readings receive higher scores. They describe conditions, not cheapness. The Buffett Indicator is displayed for context but carries zero weight because of its annual frequency and long publication lag.

3. Point-in-time data

A backtest is only honest if it uses data that was actually available at the time. Each observation has a period date and an effective date. The effective date adds a conservative publication lag; a score is never allowed to use a future revision. The newest preliminary CAPE month is excluded until its lag has elapsed.

4. Score bands

ScoreLabelInterpretation
0–20Very OvervaluedHistorically expensive or stretched conditions.
20–40OvervaluedLess favorable than the long-run middle.
40–60NeutralMixed or middling conditions.
60–80UndervaluedMore favorable than the long-run middle.
80–100Very UndervaluedHistorically cheap or fearful conditions.

Bands are descriptive. A “Very Overvalued” reading can persist, and an “Undervalued” reading can be followed by further declines.

5. Coverage and confidence

  • Coverage is the weighted share of configured indicators that are available.
  • High confidence requires coverage of at least 90% and no data-quality flags.
  • Medium confidence requires coverage of at least 70%.
  • Low confidence covers everything else, including any composite that fails the minimum gates.

Data-quality flags are not cosmetic. They include unavailable indicators, proxy inputs, survivorship bias, short percentile windows, and direct-scale components. The dashboard lists them next to the score.

6. Validation

The project computes forward returns after historical scores and publishes the results instead of promising predictive power. The validation output covers 3-month, 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year horizons for price and total return, both nominal and real. It reports the number of observations, mean and median returns, the share of positive outcomes, and Spearman rank correlation between the score and subsequent returns.

Validation is a diagnostic, not a guarantee. Overlapping windows reduce the effective sample size, the data has survivorship and revision issues, and a single market history is not a laboratory. Read the JSON directly: /data/validation.json.

7. Known limitations

  • CAPE uses current-vintage history; historical revisions are not captured.
  • Forward P/E and CNN Fear & Greed require licensed/authorized feeds and may be unavailable.
  • The ERP is a trailing earnings-yield proxy when forward earnings are unavailable.
  • Breadth uses the current constituent list for the whole history, creating survivorship bias.
  • The equal-weight relative series starts with RSP’s 2003 inception; shorter windows are flagged.
  • Yahoo Finance’s chart interface is unofficial; review its terms before commercial use.
  • Percentile windows are rolling and can change as old observations enter or leave.
  • The composite is not risk-adjusted and does not model taxes, fees, or individual goals.

8. Reproducibility

The weights file, scoring code, and validation output are in the public repository. Each snapshot includes a short weights hash so you can confirm which configuration produced it. The current configuration version is 1.0.0.
